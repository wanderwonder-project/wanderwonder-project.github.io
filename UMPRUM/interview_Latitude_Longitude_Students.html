Could you briefly tell us about this workshop Latitude Longitude?

KTThe project had two parts: the first one took place in the Czech Republic in the Krkonoše area and the second one in Fleurville, near Geneva. The point of the workshops was to get to know each other, go to a place, and create something together there to capture the essence of the place.

How was the experience of leaving the classrooms and working in nature?

VSI think for all of us it felt like a breath of fresh air, because we're so used to the routine and spending so much time in the classroom, especially Swiss students. We spend at least 8 hours a day at school. It felt really nice to be in nature, but it also felt like going outside your comfort zone. Working outside required a different kind of creativity. A lot of people embraced that and tried to detach completely from computers and just go with it.

DNIt was a really nice experience because it's always nice to go outside of school and be somewhere in nature. It was also great to meet new people from different schools, see different approaches, and learn from each other. There were also some small clashes between what we learned and what we knew, which was interesting. During the first workshop, we were still figuring out how to work together, but by the second workshop everything went smoothly. Everybody already knew each other from day one. It was so refreshing, I'd say it was amazing.

SLBWhat was really special about this collaboration is that it happened in two volumes, which is quite rare. Just having UMPRUM come to Switzerland, or us going there is already a great experience. But we could really see how much easier it was to create together the second time, how much better it flowed. Maybe there was less nervousness, less of a need to prove yourself in a way. By the second or third day of the workshop in Fleurville, everyone did a pre-presentation of what they had done so far, and you could really see how everything was coming together really nicely. So having it happen over 2 volumes was really special, I felt really lucky to be part of it.

Over the course of these two workshops, you worked and lived closely with students from another school and country. Did you notice any differences between the two schools or in their broader cultural contexts?

VSThese two volumes felt different. In the first one, we were still getting to know each other, and it felt a bit intimidating. The Czech students were so close to each other and many of them were much older than us. Most of our classmates are around 21 or 22 years old, so we felt like little babies. There was maybe an imbalance in experience and in our approach to work.

There was a kind of gray area when it came to communication. I speak Czech, so I could talk to the Czech students, but I know that some Swiss students had difficulty being heard in the first workshop. It was hard for them to speak up and propose ideas, so there was an imbalance of opinions within the groups. But that's also why the second workshop went so well, because we’d already figured out how to communicate with each other.

DNOur school (UMPRUM Prague) is a little bit individualistic, we’re taught to be quite direct about saying what we think, and if we don't like something, we’ll say it. That’s very normal for us. Sometimes it was too much for the Swiss students because we can be a bit too pushy in some ways. But it's mainly because we’re so close as a group that we’re not afraid to speak up, we are really talking about work, not about not liking each other.

Then there was the drinking part which is really normal in Czech culture—to have drinks with your teachers. That was definitely a cultural difference, but it's not really about the schools. It was more about Czech life in general.

VSYes, when we went to Prague for the first workshop, we were staying in a Pivovar which is a beer brewery, we were basically drinking every day. So that was interesting.

IMI think the first workshop in Prague kind of shifted my perspective on that. I felt more comfortable with teachers afterward. It influenced us in a good way.

SLBIn Switzerland, or at least in our school, there's a lot more formality. People tend to avoid being too direct because you don't want to hurt people's feelings, kind of tiptoeing around things. So there were definitely two cultures coming together, and when that happens, misunderstandings are natural because each side is coming from a different perspective.

How many days did you spend together, and what did the workshop days look like?

KTIt was five days. The first day was always just arriving at the place, and then we had an evening program. In the first workshop, it was speed dating, and in France we just chilled. The second day was a special program tailored to the place: in Czechia, we went hiking in the mountains, and in France, we went kayaking because it was about the river. So we always had a part dedicated to getting to know the place before we started working.

Then we had two full working days, and we worked on the project from morning till evening. On Friday, we went back to the city, to the school, for the exhibition, and there was a party on the weekend.

How was your working process? We saw that you have lots of documentation from those places. How did you find inspiration, and how did it relate to the place?

VSIt really depends on each group, because everyone approached their themes differently. For example, Susana, David, and I worked together in Fleurville. We decided to avoid computers or anything technical completely. Since we were working with the river, we experimented for three days straight with floating compositions. We collected leaves and other things we found on the ground, following our intuition and trying to make something out of them. It was very experimental in the sense that we didn’t really know what we were doing, and the result kind of appeared with the process.

IMIn Czechia, even before we formed groups, we were already collecting visual material during the hike. We took photographs and made stamps of anything we saw potential in: textures, logos, or typography. Then we realized that the other group had done the exact same thing, but with a portable scanner. It was a happy coincidence. We were able to combine both the visuals and the techniques into one object, building it from different things.

We used the same principle while working with the same team in Fleurville. We collected shells from the water, we wanted to work with them but didn't really know how. So we did a lot of research, but in parallel explored the area, the old distillery, where we found a lot of documentation in the attic about the history of the place and its previous owner. We wanted to incorporate that into the project and find a way to bring the two subjects together.

VSMost of the groups were inspired just by the nature, the collection of materials, and the history of places. On the mountain, many people researched legends and local stories. There was also the political side, the mountain sits on the border of Poland and Czechia, which opened up the whole thread of ideas. In Fleurville, it was the personal story of the owners.

SLBFor the second workshop, a local guide gave us a tour of the area and shared facts about its natural history. He told us how the area floods very often, but this actually preserves nature because it limits construction and agriculture. So our group decided to use the idea of flooding as a way to explore floating compositions on puddles, the river, and a pond. That's another way the natural history of the place ends up influencing you. But it was all very intuitive and carefree, we didn't open a computer until the day before we had to submit something, which was really nice.

For the first project, the outcome was posters, and for the second it was a zine. How was that decided, or was it suggested by the school?

KTFor the first workshop, our studio heads decided the format: we had to deliver a black-and-white poster, and we wanted to use a big roll of paper. You could exhibit anything you made, but mainly posters. Some people made a zine, we made little pieces of receipt paper for people to take. We were staying at the brewery, and there wasn't much space to work in, so we were a bit tied to the computers.

Compared to that, the second workshop in France was much more free. It was warm and sunny, so we spent a lot of time experimenting. There was more outdoor space and a big workshop area to work in. It was completely free, you could do anything, but mostly, people ended up making zines.

SLBWe were free to do whatever we wanted, but we were oriented more toward publications because it was going to be exhibited at PAGES, which is an art book fair happening at HEAD Geneva for a few years.

And actually, the aim was also to create one large, common publication summarizing both workshops together, but that wasn't going to happen at first because no one had enough time to condense all the work together. But a couple of weeks before the book fair, Christian and David wrote to us saying they wanted to come to PAGES and try to make a zine to summarize the two workshops.

KTThat's when we collected almost all the photos and material from the workshops. Our heads of the studio were supposed to make the book, but they ran out of time. So we thought if we went there, we could make the zine on the way, as we did during workshops: arriving somewhere and making something. So we decided to make the zine on the bus to Geneva. We were on the bus for 14 hours, slept for about 6 hours, and spent the other time making a zine, and then we printed it on a Risograph in Geneva. We managed to finish in time for the book fair. It was funny.

Were there any unexpected or memorable moments?

VSI think Fleurville in France left everyone with a really strong impression. The Czech and the Swiss students just kept talking about it. And when we came back to Geneva, we spent everyday together from morning to night, and we went out and ate cheese together.

SLBWhat made Fleurville really special was that we already knew each other. It was warm and nice, and the place itself—this old distillery turned art residency—had beautiful grounds and lots of different materials to work with. There were people cooking amazing food. Together, all of that made a really strong impression. Honestly, I didn't want to go back to routine after that. It felt like a little vacation where we were just having a lot of fun and not worrying about real life.

But also, we're only representing two groups out of the whole workshop, so we're probably giving a biased view. We're the people who’ve stayed most in contact from both classes. I think it also depends on how much you invest. So if you're looking to connect and go beyond the cultural differences, you’ll find people who are also trying to do the same.

While being in these locations, how was your everyday living situation? Did you have any roles like cooking every day?

VSWell, both in Prague and in Fleurville, we had someone cooking for us. So we didn't have any role in that sense. We were very lucky because the only thing we had to think about was the work.

KTAnd when we were going to open the first beer.

[everyone laughs]

VSIn Fleurville, it felt more personal because we could go into the kitchen, help out, or cook our own things. During the week, we had 3 birthdays, so we made some cakes, tiramisu, and brownies. In Prague, it was more like an official restaurant, and people were paid to cook for us.

As the final question, based on this experience, how do you imagine the future design education?

KTIt's hard to reflect on those two workshops separately because they have two parts: the working part and the fun part. I don’t think those can be divided. I attended another workshop last year in Brussels — smaller, around 15 people, also from different schools — and it wasn't organized well. We were just stuck in a warehouse all day, then went to our accommodation without really connecting. So it really affected the work and the outcome wasn't that good. I think it's very important to connect people from different countries and backgrounds.

VSIt's also important to keep making those workshops in nature to break the routine of working in offices or classrooms. With technology evolving, AI and everything else, going outside gives you new creativity and a new visual culture. As designers, it’s important to step out of our usual routines and detach from technology. It gives you a new approach that can carry back into your work. Both workshops were really great for that reason, being outside changed a lot of our perspectives.

SLBI completely agree. Being outside is a big factor, but also just being in a completely different context with people you haven't met. It changes all your habits of just opening your computer and going to your favorite reference website. It regenerates your creativity. For me, the second workshop was a lot of design through play, where having fun and laughing were huge parts of it.
